   # My m00corne Journey: From Zero to Comfortable Split Keyboard Bliss
   
   ## The Beginning: Why I Wanted a Split Keyboard
   
   I used to type like everyone else—hands together on a single wide keyboard. But after years of software development and writing, I started noticing the strain. My shoulders would round
   forward, my wrists would angle awkwardly, and by the end of a coding session, my neck would be tight. Sound familiar?
   
   That's when I discovered the Corne keyboard—a minimalist split keyboard design that caught my attention. "Minimalist" because it has just 42 keys instead of the 100+ on a standard 
  keyboard. "Split" because it separates left and right halves, letting each hand sit at a natural angle.
   
   The Corne seemed perfect, but there's a catch: it requires custom configuration. No plug-and-play here. You build the firmware, define your own keymap, and optimize it for *your* 
  workflow. That's where the real journey began.
   
   ## The Challenge: Learning the Corne Ecosystem
   
   When I first started, I faced a steep learning curve:
   
   * **Smaller keyboard = fewer keys**: How do I access numbers, symbols, and special characters with only 42 keys?
   * **Layers, not shift keys**: Most special characters come through *layers*—think of them as different "modes" you can activate, similar to how holding Shift gives you capital letters,
   but much more flexible.
   * **Custom firmware configuration**: I needed to learn the ZMK firmware system to build and flash my configuration.
   * **Finding my own optimal setup**: There's no "right" answer. Every user needs to discover what layers, shortcuts, and key placements work for their hands and workflow.
   
   This wasn't just about buying a keyboard—it was about understanding a different paradigm of how keyboards can work.
   
   ## The Journey: Building My Perfect Setup
   
   What's special about documenting my m00corne configuration is the iterative process. I didn't arrive at my final setup overnight. Here's what evolved:
   
   ### Phase 1: The Foundation
   
   I started with the basics—getting the keyboard to compile and flash. The first commits established:
   
   * **Board and firmware setup**: Configuring ZMK to recognize the hardware
   * **Basic keymap**: Creating a functional layout that could handle daily typing
   * **Layer structure**: Defining how I'd access less-common keys (numbers, symbols, navigation)
   
   At this stage, I had a working keyboard, but it felt awkward. Muscle memory from decades of standard keyboards doesn't transfer instantly. Every number, every arrow key, every symbol 
  required conscious thought.
   
   ### Phase 2: Optimization and Comfort
   
   Over several iterations, I refined:
   
   * **Key placement**: Moving frequently-used keys to easier positions
   * **Layer organization**: Discovering which keys to group together made the most sense
   * **Speed improvements**: Adding layer-tap and mod-tap features (keys that do one thing when tapped, another when held)
   * **Ergonomic adjustments**: Fine-tuning which fingers should access which layers
   
   This is where the real improvement came. Small tweaks—moving the space bar to a thumb key, adjusting layer modifiers, reorganizing number placement—transformed my experience from "I 
  can type" to "this feels natural."
   
   ### Phase 3: Advanced Features
   
   As I grew comfortable, I added:
   
   * **Macros**: Quick shortcuts for common coding patterns or commands
   * **Combo keys**: Two keys pressed together trigger a third key or command
   * **Custom behaviors**: Advanced ZMK features that made my workflow faster
   * **Refined layout**: Stabilizing a keymap that worked with my habits, not against them
   
   Each improvement made a measurable difference in speed and comfort. What started as "this is different" became "I can't imagine using a standard keyboard anymore."
   
   ## The Results: Why It Was Worth It
   
   After settling on my final configuration, the benefits were clear:
   
   * **Reduced strain**: My shoulders, wrists, and neck no longer ache after long sessions. The split design lets my hands sit at a natural, relaxed angle.
   * **Faster typing**: Once muscle memory built up, my typing speed actually increased. The optimized layer system keeps frequently-used keys at my fingertips.
   * **Genuine comfort**: The keyboard now feels like an extension of my hands, not something I'm fighting against.
   * **Personal workflow**: My keymap is built specifically for how *I* work, not how keyboard manufacturers think people should work.
   
   Beyond personal benefits, I discovered something bigger: a keyboard can be a tool for learning. The process of designing your own keymap teaches you about ergonomics, efficiency, and 
  intentional design.
   
   ## Lessons Learned: What I'd Tell My Past Self
   
   ### 1. Give It Time
   
   The first week was frustrating. The second week, slightly less so. By week three, things clicked. Don't expect instant gratification—expect a learning curve that pays off.
   
   ### 2. Small Changes Matter
   
   A single key repositioned or a new layer organized differently can make a huge difference. You don't need a complete redesign; incremental improvements compound.
   
   ### 3. Copy, Then Customize
   
   I started by studying existing keymaps from the Corne community. Understanding how others solved the problem gave me a foundation to build my own. You don't need to invent everything 
  from scratch.
   
   ### 4. Document Your Choices
   
   Every configuration file, every layer, every macro—document *why* you chose it. This helps you remember your reasoning if you need to troubleshoot later.
   
   ### 5. There's No "Final" Version
   
   My configuration has been updated many times, and it will continue to evolve. As my workflows change or as I discover new ZMK features, I adjust. That's not a failure—it's the point.
   
   ## For You: What This Means if You're Curious
   
   If you're interested in split keyboards or the Corne specifically, here's what my journey suggests:
   
   * **It's learnable**: You don't need to be a hardware engineer or firmware expert. If you can follow documentation and are willing to experiment, you can build a working Corne setup.
   * **It's worth the effort**: The discomfort reduction and workflow improvements justify the learning curve. Your hands will thank you.
   * **The community helps**: The Corne community is active, friendly, and willing to share configurations. You're not alone in this journey.
   * **You can start small**: You don't need perfect ergonomics from day one. Start with a basic, functional setup and refine from there.
   
   ## The Bottom Line
   
   My m00corne journey transformed how I work. It wasn't just about swapping keyboards—it was about taking control of a tool I use for 8+ hours a day and shaping it to fit my hands, not 
  the other way around.
   
   If you spend hours at a keyboard and have ever experienced strain or discomfort, the Corne (and split keyboards in general) offer a different path. Yes, there's a learning curve. But 
  for me, it was one of the best technical investments I've made.
   
   The keyboard sitting on my desk right now is the result of dozens of small improvements, experiments, and iterations. Every key is placed intentionally. Every layer serves a purpose. 
  And most importantly, every session is genuinely comfortable.
   
   That's the journey. That's what makes m00corne special—not just the final configuration, but the process of thoughtfully building something that works for you.
